# The Pitch

> **What this is:** the agency presentation. An account executive's pitch for the
> line, and a copy chief's build for the campaign that makes it ship.
>
> It sits *under* [`campaign.md`](campaign.md), which stays the operating
> document — three pillars, named owners, dated cadence. This doesn't change any
> of that. It gives it a spine to hang on, because right now the plan knows
> exactly what to do every day and doesn't have a sentence.

---

## PART ONE — THE PITCH

*Lights down. One slide, and it's blank.*

---

Three-fourteen in the morning.

You're at the desk. Two monitors, and only one of them matters. There's a candle
forming on a four-hour chart and you have been watching it develop for something
like forty minutes, which is a long time to look at a rectangle.

And here's the thing — you have thoughts about it. Good ones. You've been right
about this kind of setup before, and you know why, and you could explain it in
about ninety seconds to anybody who asked.

Nobody's asked.

So you do what everyone does. You open the app and you type it out, and you sit
there with your thumb on the button, and you post it into the feed. And the feed
does what the feed does.

Nothing.

Not a rejection. Rejection would be *something*. Just — the post goes in, and the
water closes over it, and forty seconds later somebody's dog video is where your
idea used to be.

*(beat)*

We think crypto is about money. It isn't. Anybody who's actually been in it knows
the money is a scoreboard for something else.

It's about being **early**. And being early is the loneliest thing there is,
because by definition, nobody's there yet. That's the whole word. Early means:
you, and an idea, and no room to say it in.

*(beat)*

Now. Every platform ever built has promised to fix this, and every one of them has
lied in exactly the same way. They gave you a *feed*.

A feed is a slot machine. You put a thought in, you pull the handle, and the house
decides whether anyone hears it. You don't have a spot. You have a *chance*. And
the chance is small, and it resets every morning, and you're back at the machine.

Television never lied to you about this, because television never let you in at
all. ESPN doesn't have an open call. You cannot claim eleven o'clock.

*(beat)*

We built the third thing.

CSGN is a television network that runs twenty-four hours a day. There's a
schedule — a real one, twelve blocks, every day, the same way there's been a
schedule since 1948.

And the schedule has holes in it.

Not by accident. **On purpose.** Eight blocks a day, open. And the way you get
one is: you take it.

That's it. That's the whole mechanism. You connect an account, you claim an hour,
and at three o'clock this afternoon your face and your name and your ninety
seconds about that four-hour chart go out on a channel — with a lower third, and a
ticker, and a bug in the corner, on a network with a ratings book.

Nobody voted on whether you're interesting. Nobody's algorithm weighed you. The
hour was empty, and you took it, and now it's yours.

*(long beat)*

And on the other side of it — at three-fourteen in the morning, when you're at
that desk with your rectangle and your forty minutes — there's somebody on.

There's *always* somebody on. That's what a network is. It's the promise that the
room is never empty, at any hour, forever.

*(He turns the card over.)*

Two words. It's what the floor director says when the light goes red and it's
your turn. It's what you say when somebody makes a bet and you're taking it.

It's the only thing anybody in this business has ever actually wanted to hear.

## PART TWO — PEGGY'S TURN

*She's been quiet the whole pitch. She has notes.*

---

It's the line. I'm not arguing about the line.

I'm going to tell you why it's the line, because the reason matters more than the
line does, and if we get the reason wrong we'll spend six months making beautiful
work for the wrong thing.

**"You're on" is not a slogan. It's a promise with a timestamp.**

Every other crypto brand writes a line that's an adjective. *The future of
whatever.* Unfalsifiable, so it costs nothing, so it means nothing. Ours can be
checked. Somebody reads it, goes to the site, and either there's an empty hour at
3 PM with a button on it — or we're liars.

That's terrifying and it's the best thing about it. **We are the only brand in
this category whose tagline can be disproven by Tuesday.** That's not a risk to
manage, it's the entire strategic advantage, and every piece of work we make has
to protect it.

Which gives me the one rule for this campaign:

> ### The empty slot is the creative.
>
> We don't make ads about the network. We publish the inventory, and the inventory
> sells itself. Every day, one graphic: today's schedule, one hour highlighted,
> nobody's name on it.
>
> **"4 PM ET. Nobody's claimed it. You're on."**

That post is free, it takes ninety seconds, it can run every day for five years,
and it never needs an idea. It's a want ad for a job nobody knew existed. And the
day it stops being true — the day every hour is taken — that's not the campaign
failing. **That's the campaign's greatest possible ad**, and we run *that*: the
full board, every hour claimed, and one line underneath.

*"Full. Come back tomorrow at 3 AM."*

---

### 1. Three audiences, one line, three different meanings

The reason we don't need three campaigns is that the line already means three
different things depending on who's reading it. Don't explain this to anyone. Just
make sure each channel is aimed at one of them.

| Who | What "YOU'RE ON" means to them | Where they hear it |
|---|---|---|
| **The creator** — 200 followers, real opinions, no room | *There's a slot with your name on it.* | X replies, Twitch, Discord, the daily open-slot post |
| **The viewer** — up at 3 AM, alone | *Somebody's broadcasting. Right now.* | Clips, Shorts, the live post, the schedule |
| **The holder** — owns the token | *Your bag is voting. You decide who's on.* | The Draft, The Floor, the app |

That third one is the one people will underestimate, and it's the one that makes
this a *network* instead of a channel. The holder doesn't just watch — they
program it. Every hour, they pick who goes on ([`token-voting.md`](token-voting.md)).
When we say "you're on" to a holder, we mean *you're the one who decides*.

---

### 2. The proof stack — three things that make it real, in order

A network is a *claim*. Anybody can say they're a network. Three artifacts prove
it, and they should be built and pushed in this order because each one makes the
next one believable.

**1. THE SCHEDULE.** A real one, published, with real names on real hours and real
holes in between. This already exists. It is the single most under-used asset in
the entire company. **Post it every single day.** Not when there's news. Daily. A
schedule that appears daily is a network; a schedule that appears when convenient
is a website.

**2. THE DRAFT.** Every hour, on the half-hour, the network holds an election for
who goes on next, live, with a countdown, and the audience decides
([`token-voting.md`](token-voting.md) §3). This is the campaign's daily event and
its best possible piece of content, because it is *appointment television about
appointment television*. Sixteen times a day, something happens at a known time
and there's a result. Nothing else in crypto has that.

**3. THE BOOK.** Ratings ([`csgn-share.md`](csgn-share.md)). Monthly, published,
covering the whole category — not just us. This is the one that changes what we
*are*. The moment a competitor screenshots their own row to brag about it, we
stopped being a channel and became the body that keeps the record. **Networks that
publish the ratings outlive networks that are in them.**

Notice all three already exist as plans or code. This campaign requires almost no
new invention. It requires *publishing on a clock*, which is harder.

---

### 3. Platform by platform — what each one is actually for

The mistake would be posting the same thing five places. Each of these does one
job. If a channel doesn't have a job, we don't post there.

**X — the home field.** Two accounts, two jobs, and they must not blur
([`master-plan.md`](master-plan.md) §13.2):

- **@CSGNet is the network.** It never has opinions. It posts the schedule, the
  Draft result, the live post, the Overnight ratings, the Book. It reads like a
  wire service. Boring on purpose — utilities are boring, and that's why people
  trust them.
- **The founder account is the theorist.** StreamFi, ACM, the ideas. Never the
  pitch. Per `campaign.md` §2.1, this is the single biggest behavioral edge
  available, and the fastest way to lose it is to let the network account develop
  a personality or the founder account develop a sales quota.

**Twitch — the recruiting ground, not the audience.** We're not trying to build a
following on Twitch. We're trying to *find the 200-follower streamer with real
opinions and no room*, and hand them a network hour. Every open-slot post is a
recruiting ad. The pitch to them is one sentence and it isn't about the token:
*"You'll stream to a lower third, a ticker and a ratings book instead of to
nobody."*

**TikTok / Reels / Shorts — the clip engine.** Volume, verticals, no strategy
beyond consistency and the partner's clip pipeline (`campaign.md` §2.2). The one
rule: **every clip ends on the schedule.** The clip is the hook; the empty hour is
the ask.

**YouTube — the library.** Full VODs, named and numbered, because it's the only
platform where something posted in March is still working in December. This is
where "a network with an archive" stops being a metaphor.

**Discord — the notification spine** (§9). The Draft is the killer use case: a
ping thirty minutes before every open hour, *"the board is up, 5 candidates,
voting closes at 3:00."* That's a reason to have notifications on that no other
crypto Discord has.

**Farcaster — the theory audience.** Where StreamFi and ACM get argued about
rather than announced. Low volume, high quality, founder only.

### 4. The first thirty days

Nothing here needs a budget. Everything here needs a clock. The point of month one
is not reach — it's to establish that we publish on a schedule, because that's the
only thing that makes anyone believe the schedule.

**Week 1 — Prove the clock.**
- The daily open-slot graphic starts. Every day. Non-negotiable, including the
  days it feels pointless.
- The ratings poller goes live and starts collecting silently
  ([`csgn-share.md`](csgn-share.md) §7). Publish nothing yet. **Every day it isn't
  running is a day of history that can't be recovered.**
- Founder account: the "you're shouting into a slot machine" thesis post. That's
  the pitch above, written down, with no product in it until the last line.

**Week 2 — Land the line.**
- "YOU'RE ON" goes into the bio, the site header, the stream's end card, the
  offline panel, and the last frame of every clip. One line, everywhere, in the
  same words. Consistency is the entire mechanism by which a line becomes a brand.
- First claim story: a real streamer, a real hour, a real clip of them on air.
  *"He claimed 3 PM on a Tuesday."* This is the campaign's first piece of proof
  and it's worth waiting for a good one.

**Week 3 — Turn on the event.**
- The Draft runs on one hour a day (per `token-voting.md` §5 — one hour first,
  never all eight on day one).
- Discord pings it. X posts the board at T-30 and the result at T-0.
- The Floor goes on air during the vote — the hundred-seat graphic, filling live.

**Week 4 — Show the homework.**
- The first Overnight post: yesterday's Prime share, one graphic, ~9 AM ET.
- Publish the methodology page and the competitive set *before* anyone asks
  ([`csgn-share.md`](csgn-share.md) §2.1). Publishing the denominator unprompted
  is the whole credibility play.
- The number will be small. **Post it anyway.** A ratings book that starts
  reporting once the numbers got good is a ratings book nobody believes.

---

### 5. What we are not doing

Half of a campaign is the list of things you refuse, so here it is, and it should
be enforced against ourselves harder than against anyone else.

- **No airdrop, no points program, no farming.** Every one of them buys a crowd
  that leaves on the same day. We're selling a slot on a schedule; if that isn't
  worth showing up for, no amount of points fixes it.
- **No paid KOL threads.** The audience can identify these on sight and the only
  thing they reliably communicate is that we had to pay someone.
- **No price talk from the network account.** Ever. @CSGNet reports the schedule
  and the ratings. A network that talks about its own stock price is not a network.
- **No "utility" language.** Nobody has ever wanted utility. They want a slot, a
  vote, and a scoreboard, and we should say those words instead.
- **No pitching in someone else's room** (`campaign.md` §2.1). The room is worth
  more than any conversion it could produce.
- **Never miss the daily post.** A network that skips a day is a website. This is
  the only rule on the list that is genuinely unforgiving, and it's the one the
  whole line depends on.

---

### 6. How we know it worked

Not impressions. Not followers. Four numbers, in order of how much they mean:

| # | Metric | Why it's the one | 90-day target |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Open hours claimed / open hours available** | The line's literal truth value. This *is* "YOU'RE ON," measured | 60% |
| 2 | **Repeat claimants** | Anyone can be talked into one hour. Coming back means the product worked | 40% claim a 2nd |
| 3 | **CSGN Share, Prime** | Are we winning the hours that are hard to win | 15 share |
| 4 | **Draft turnout (% of supply)** | Whether holders actually program the network or just hold it | 25% |

Every one of these is a number we generate, publish, and can be held to. That's
deliberate. **A campaign built on a promise with a timestamp has to report against
a clock**, and reporting honestly on a small number is what earns the right to
report a big one later.

*The proof is that at 3 PM today, an hour on a real television network is empty,
it has a button on it, and anybody reading this can take it.*

*Everything else is production.*
